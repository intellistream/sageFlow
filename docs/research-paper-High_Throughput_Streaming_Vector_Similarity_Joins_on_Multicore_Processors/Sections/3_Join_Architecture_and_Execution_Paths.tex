\section{Core Join Architecture and Execution Paths}
\label{sec:core-architecture}

Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement} separates correctness semantics from coverage/control policies. Following that separation, this section reframes our VSJoin design around three mechanism-level goals that correspond to propositions P1--P3 in Section~\ref{sec:intro}: maintaining quality under update churn, improving boundary coverage under bounded fanout, and stabilizing skew without heavy foreground synchronization. We present each mechanism in a consistent \textit{Goal $\rightarrow$ Mechanism $\rightarrow$ Trade-off} form so that later implementation and results sections can map one-to-one to testable claims.

\subsection{Execution Contract and Design Goals}

For an arriving record $x$ from side $s \in \{L,R\}$, VSJoin follows an eager pipeline: route, state update, candidate retrieval, verification, and emission. A pair $(x,y)$ is emitted only if
\[
|x.\mathit{ts} - y.\mathit{ts}| \le W \quad \land \quad \mathrm{sim}(x,y) \ge \theta.
\]
Hence, correctness remains anchored in verification, while routing/index/control policies determine coverage and cost.

We organize the design around three goals:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{G1 (P1):} keep foreground insert/probe paths short while bounding index staleness.
    \item \textbf{G2 (P2):} improve boundary-match coverage without paying full broadcast cost.
    \item \textbf{G3 (P3):} react to skew with predictable control overhead and lock-light reads.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mechanism I: Bounded-Staleness Read/Write Decoupling (for G1)}

\paragraph{Design goal.}
VSJoin must sustain high-rate insert/expire/probe traffic under sliding windows. A single shared structure tends to force a poor compromise: either write-heavy contention or stale read views.

\paragraph{Mechanism sketch.}
VSJoin uses two-tier indexing with distinct responsibilities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Local mutable tier} for update-dominant hot-path inserts and partition-local probes.
    \item \textbf{Global read-optimized tier} for broader retrieval coverage, refreshed in a periodic background loop.
\end{itemize}
For query record $x$, candidate sets are merged by identity:
\[
\mathcal{C}(x)=\mathrm{Dedup}\big(\mathcal{C}_{\text{local}}(x)\cup\mathcal{C}_{\text{global}}(x)\big).
\]
In our implementation, global refresh is periodic (not event-driven) and combined with event-time validity filtering, so semantically stale snapshots are not repeatedly rebuilt.

\paragraph{Bounded-staleness contract and control knobs.}
Let $t_{\mathrm{pub}}$ be the publish time of the latest global snapshot used at processing time $t$, and define snapshot age
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{snap}}(t)=t-t_{\mathrm{pub}}.
\]
With rebuild interval $T_{\mathrm{rebuild}}$ and bounded scheduler jitter $\delta_{\mathrm{sched}}$, VSJoin targets
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{snap}}(t) \le T_{\mathrm{rebuild}} + \delta_{\mathrm{sched}}
\]
for the global tier under normal operation. This is a \emph{coverage-side} freshness budget, not a direct correctness guarantee: final output remains governed by window-time and similarity verification. The main control knobs are (i) rebuild interval $T_{\mathrm{rebuild}}$ and (ii) event-time validity filtering aggressiveness on snapshot materialization.

\paragraph{Expected metric effects (P1 mapping).}
Reducing $T_{\mathrm{rebuild}}$ usually improves global-tier freshness and can recover recall under rapid state churn, but increases background rebuild work and may raise p99 latency due to shared resource pressure. Increasing $T_{\mathrm{rebuild}}$ reduces maintenance overhead and can improve foreground throughput, but may widen freshness lag and hurt recall on fast-drifting windows. Therefore, P1 is evaluated as a bounded trade-off among throughput, p99 latency, and recall rather than a single-metric optimum.

\noindent\textbf{Proposition linkage (P1).}
This mechanism yields a testable claim: periodic global refresh with explicit staleness budgeting improves throughput--latency behavior under update churn while preserving quality in evaluated settings.

\paragraph{Trade-off and scope.}
Read/write decoupling introduces bounded staleness controlled by refresh cadence, in exchange for lower foreground synchronization cost. This mechanism does not claim zero staleness; it claims a controllable freshness--overhead trade-off under evaluated workloads.

\subsection{Mechanism II: Budgeted Boundary Coverage Routing (for G2)}

\paragraph{Design goal.}
As parallelism increases, each partition covers a smaller locality region, so near-boundary vectors are more likely to have true neighbors in adjacent partitions. Strict unicast is efficient but increasingly risks boundary misses; full broadcast improves coverage but inflates routing and compute cost. VSJoin therefore targets a budgeted middle path with explicit boundary fanout control.

\paragraph{Mechanism sketch.}
VSJoin exposes three routing strategies as a unified comparison frame: strict unicast ($|\mathcal{P}(x)|=1$), budgeted multicast ($1<|\mathcal{P}(x)|\le B$), and broadcast ($|\mathcal{P}(x)|=P$), where $\mathcal{P}(x)$ is the routed physical-partition set for record $x$, $P$ is total physical partitions, and $B$ is a configurable fanout budget. In evaluated runs, the budgeted policy is constrained by
\[
\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{P}(x)|] \le B, \quad 1 \le B \le P,
\]
with larger $B$ increasing expected boundary coverage at higher routing cost.

VSJoin realizes this policy in two stages. First, LSH-based physical routing chooses one or multiple candidate physical partitions, where non-boundary vectors stay mostly unicast and boundary vectors consume fanout budget for bounded multicast. Second, each selected physical partition is expanded into logical partitions through virtualization:
\[
\texttt{logical\_pid}=\texttt{physical\_pid}\times V + h(\texttt{uid})\bmod V.
\]
This division of labor is deliberate: physical routing provides semantic neighborhood coverage, while logical virtualization provides finer load-control and remapping granularity without changing similarity semantics.

To prevent duplicate outputs from inflated fanout, VSJoin applies identity-based deduplication before final verification/emission:
\[
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{route}}(x)=\mathrm{Dedup}\Big(\bigcup_{p\in \mathcal{P}(x)} \mathcal{C}_p(x)\Big).
\]

\paragraph{Trade-off and scope.}
Budgeted routing makes the recall--overhead trade-off explicit rather than implicit. When $B$ is too small, boundary coverage is under-provisioned and recall degrades toward unicast behavior; when $B$ is too large, duplicate candidates and routing/compute overhead grow, approaching broadcast-like inefficiency. In our implementation, this mechanism is scoped to selecting a controllable operating region under evaluated workloads, not claiming universally optimal fanout.

\noindent\textbf{Proposition linkage (P2).}
This mechanism yields a testable claim: bounded multicast under explicit fanout budgets improves quality--cost trade-offs compared with unicast-only and full-broadcast extremes in evaluated workloads.

\subsection{Mechanism III: Predictable Control Plane for Skew (for G3)}

\paragraph{Design goal.}
Skew and workload drift can make static partition-to-worker mapping unstable over time. VSJoin therefore targets a control plane with two properties: lock-light reads on the foreground path and bounded per-round adaptation cost on the background path.

\paragraph{Mechanism sketch.}
VSJoin decouples logical partition identity from worker placement through an AssignmentTable indirection. The table uses RCU-style double buffering: readers consult the current pointer without locking, while writers apply a batch update on the inactive copy and publish it atomically.

The LoadMonitor reports sampled per-subtask signals and maintains both cumulative and smoothed telemetry. In our implementation, rebalance decisions are driven primarily by interval load,
\[
\ell_i = \Delta \mathrm{records}_i + \beta \cdot \mathrm{backlog}_i,
\]
with imbalance ratio
\[
\rho = \frac{\max_i \ell_i}{\frac{1}{P}\sum_{i=1}^{P} \ell_i}.
\]
When $\rho \ge \tau$, VSJoin remaps at most $M$ logical partitions in one round (from overloaded toward less-loaded workers), then atomically publishes the new AssignmentTable.

In the current implementation, remapping updates routing metadata for future records and does not perform synchronous full-state migration on the foreground join path.

\paragraph{Trade-off and scope.}
This control plane improves robustness to skew with predictable per-round overhead bounds, but it is heuristic rather than globally optimal at each instant. Effectiveness depends on sampling quality, control interval, and workload drift speed; under abrupt drift, temporary imbalance can persist until subsequent control rounds.

\noindent\textbf{Proposition linkage (P3).}
This mechanism yields a testable claim: periodic, threshold-triggered, bounded remapping with atomic publication improves load stability while keeping control overhead bounded and foreground routing reads lock-light under evaluated workloads.

\subsection{Integrated VSJoin Path and Baseline Context}

The integrated VSJoin path combines the three mechanisms as one pipeline: bounded-staleness indexing for foreground/background decoupling, budgeted boundary coverage for candidate routing, and predictable control-plane remapping for skew adaptation. These mechanisms are complementary: removing any one of them pushes the system toward a known failure mode (contention/staleness, boundary miss, or skew persistence).

BruteForce, IVF, HNSW, HDR-Tree, LSH path, ClusteredJoin, and S3J-style path are included as baselines under a shared runtime substrate. They provide boundary references for cost/quality behavior, while the primary narrative remains VSJoin mechanisms and their proposition-level evidence.

The next sections implement this mapping explicitly: Section~\ref{sec:implementation-details} aligns code-level semantics with the three mechanisms, and Section~\ref{sec:results} evaluates P1--P3 with scope-qualified conclusions.
